**Quick answer:** Khatarua (also written Khatadua or Khatarwa) is the Kumaoni festival that marks the end of the monsoon and the arrival of autumn. It falls on the **first day of Asoj (Ashwin)** — **Thursday, 17 September in 2026**. Bonfires are lit at dusk, an effigy of Khatarua is burnt on them, cucumbers are thrown into the flames, and cattle are washed, fed and honoured. Children circle the fire chanting *"Gaay ki jeet, Khatarwe ki haar"* — the cow has won, Khatarua has lost.

If you drive through the Almora hills on a mid-September evening you can see them from a long way off: small fires on the ridges, one per village, scattered like stars the wrong way up. That is Khatarua, and it is the least documented of the major Kumaoni festivals — partly because it has never been a temple festival or a town festival. It happens in a village courtyard or a crossroads, and if the village is empty it does not happen at all.

## What Khatarua Is

Khatarua falls on **Asoj Sankranti**, the day the sun enters Virgo and the solar month of Asoj (Ashwin) begins. Like [Harela](/festivals/harela), [Ghee Sankranti](/festivals/ghee-sankranti) and [Phool Dei](/festivals/phool-dei), it is a solar date, which is why it barely moves — mid-September, every year.

What it marks is a genuine turn. By mid-September in Kumaon the monsoon has broken. The paddy is heavy in the terraces, the ground is still damp but no longer saturated, the nights have gone cold enough to notice, and the sky sharpens to the blue that holds until winter. The animals have spent three months in wet sheds. The festival is the moment the household says out loud that the wet season is finished and turns to face the harvest.

It is best understood as a **pastoral festival** rather than a religious one. There is no deity at the centre of it. There is a fire, a herd, and a season.

## The Bonfire and the Effigy

Through the day, children collect fuel — dry pine needles, twigs, wild grass, nettle stalks, cut fodder. Households add to the heap whatever has gone useless over the year: broken furniture, waste paper, worn-out things. The pile is built at a crossroads or in the central village ground, and an **effigy of Khatarua** is set on top of it.

At dusk the heap is lit. Children circle the flames waving flags — this is a flag festival as much as a fire festival — and beat at the fire with sticks dressed with flowers and pieces of cucumber, singing as the effigy goes up. The common chant, in one of its Almora forms, runs *"Gaay ki jeet, Khatarwe ki haar, bhaag Khatarua dhaar-e-dhaar"*: the cow has won, Khatarua is beaten, run Khatarua, ridge after ridge.

Two things happen after the fire dies down. **Ash is smeared** on foreheads and on the walls of the cowsheds. And **torches are carried from the main fire back to each house's cowshed** — to drive out the damp of three months and, in the way it is explained locally, to drive out the evil eye with it. The practical and the ritual are not separable here; a cowshed that has been smoked out after a monsoon is genuinely a healthier cowshed.

Jumping the flames is said to give courage and health. A bigger fire is said to guard the cattle better. Nobody argues about the mechanism.

## The Cucumber

The detail everyone remembers from childhood is the cucumber. Ripe monsoon cucumbers — and, in many villages, whatever else the season has given: guavas, pears, apples, maize — are thrown into the Khatarua fire. Some are eaten straight out of the embers, some carried home.

The offering is understood as destroying evil influence. It is also, plainly, the first surplus of the year being put into a fire in front of everyone, which is what a harvest festival is for. What comes out of the fire is eaten beside it, with **maize roti**, curd, chutney and jaggery, sometimes kheer, sometimes pakodi passed around as neighbours come by.

## The Cattle Come First

Before any of the fire happens, the animals are dealt with. Sheds are swept and cleaned out. Cattle are washed. They are fed fresh green fodder, and in many households given a proper bhog — flattened rice, milk, ghee. Horns are oiled; some families garland the animals.

People in the hills will tell you Khatarua is the new year for the cattle, and they mean it fairly literally. Terraced hill farming does not work without bullocks. The festival's structure — animals first, then the fire that protects them, then the food — puts the herd at the centre of the household's gratitude, which is unusual even among agricultural festivals.

## The Battle Behind the Chant

The chant is about a cow beating Khatarua, and behind it sits a Chand-dynasty story that Kumaonis tell as history.

After the death of **Raja Rudra Chand**, the succession in Kumaon was disputed and the kingdom spent two years in fighting among his heirs. **Baz Bahadur Chand** eventually took the throne. The Garhwal forces used the disorder to invade, and villagers appealed to the new king for protection. He sent his best men against the fort at **Chandpur Garhi**, but the fort sat on a hilltop and the attackers were cut down as they climbed.

The king then changed the method. He sent his soldiers in **driving a herd of cows before them, carrying flags marked with the image of a cow**. The Garhwali defenders — for whom the cow is equally sacred — could not fire into them. The fort fell. Afterwards, cucumbers were shared among the soldiers as a rough field meal, and bonfires were lit on the ridges to carry news of the victory back to Almora, the capital.

That is the account given for the cucumber, the flags, the cow, and the fires — and for the name in the chant. It should be said plainly that this is folk history rather than documented military record, and that some readings treat "Khatarua" as the burnt-out effigy of the wet season itself rather than a defeated commander. Both explanations circulate in the same villages, often from the same people.

## A Festival That Is Thinning

Khatarua is in visible decline, and the reason is not indifference. It is **migration**.

The festival needs two things a modern Kumaoni village increasingly lacks: children to gather the fuel and circle the fire, and cattle to honour. As families leave the hills for Haldwani, Delhi and beyond, and as households that stay keep fewer animals, the bonfires get smaller. Villages in Almora district that once lit a single large fire in the central ground now light something much reduced, or nothing. Journalists reporting from the Almora villages describe it plainly as a ritual fading along with the herds.

It survives in pockets. Effigies still go up, cucumbers still get roasted, torches still get carried back to the sheds, and children are still taught the chant. It is a festival that will last exactly as long as there are households in the hills keeping animals — which is a more honest thing to say about it than calling it timeless.

Unlike most Kumaoni festivals, Khatarua has no [Aipan](/aipan) tradition attached to it. It happens outdoors, at a crossroads, in the dark. Where a household draws anything at all it is a simple threshold marking of the kind covered in [the ritual significance of Aipan](/aipan/aipan-ritual-significance).

Khatarua falls two days before [Nanda Ashtami](/festivals/nanda-ashtami) in 2026, which makes mid-September the densest week in the Kumaoni year. The full year is set out in the [Kumaoni festival calendar 2026–27](/festivals/kumaoni-calendar).

## FAQ

### When is Khatarua in 2026?

Khatarua 2026 falls on Thursday, 17 September — Asoj (Ashwin) Sankranti, the first day of the solar month of Asoj. Because it is a solar date rather than a lunar one, it lands in mid-September every year.

### What is the Khatarua festival?

Khatarua is a Kumaoni festival marking the end of the monsoon and the start of autumn. Bonfires are lit at dusk with an effigy of Khatarua on top, cucumbers and seasonal produce are thrown into the flames, cattle are washed and fed, and children circle the fire with flags, singing that the cow has won and Khatarua has lost.

### Why are cucumbers thrown into the Khatarua fire?

The offering is said to destroy evil influence and protect the cattle. The custom is also linked to the Chand-dynasty story in which cucumbers were shared among soldiers after the victory at Chandpur Garhi. Roasted cucumbers are eaten at the fire or carried home.

### What is the story behind Khatarua?

The commonly told account is that Baz Bahadur Chand of Kumaon defeated the Garhwal forces at Chandpur Garhi by sending soldiers in behind a herd of cows and under cow-marked flags, which the defenders would not fire on. Bonfires on the ridges carried news of the victory to Almora. Some readings instead treat Khatarua as the effigy of the wet season, burnt to end it.

### Is Khatarua celebrated in Garhwal too?

It is overwhelmingly a Kumaoni festival, strongest in Almora and the surrounding districts, though it is observed in parts of Garhwal as well. The cow-and-Garhwal element of the legend is specifically a Kumaoni telling.
