In the Kumaoni reckoning of the year, few days are loved as plainly as Harela. The word means "the day of green," and that is exactly what it is — a festival of seedlings, sown in the dark of the house and carried out into the light, marking the turn of the season toward growth.

Harela falls at the start of Shravan, in July, as the monsoon gathers over the high ridges. For an agricultural people, the timing is everything: it marks the sowing season, the moment the year tilts from waiting into doing.

## The seven grains

Ten days before the festival, the ritual begins quietly indoors. Families fill small baskets with soil and sow seven kinds of grain — maize, barley, wheat, mustard, and pulses among them. Kept in a dim corner and watered each morning, the seedlings grow pale and tall.

By the tenth day they stand several inches high — the *harela* itself, grown by the family's own hands over ten days of small daily care.

<Callout>**Why pale, not green?** Kept away from sunlight, the seedlings grow a soft yellow-green. The blanching is intentional — it makes the cut harela tender and bright against the skin when placed as a blessing.</Callout>

## The blessing of the green

On the morning of Harela, the seedlings are cut. Elders place the cut harela on the heads and behind the ears of younger family members, offering a blessing for health and long life in the year ahead. The words spoken are simple and old, passed down without change.

Where many festivals mark themselves with fire or feast, Harela's central act is a sprig of green tucked behind a child's ear by a grandmother's hand.

## The gods of the green festival

Harela is especially associated with Shiva and Parvati. In many homes small clay figures called *dikara* are fashioned for the occasion — modelled by hand, painted, and worshipped as part of the household rite. In some traditions the day commemorates the marriage of Shiva and Parvati.

## What is eaten, and what is drawn

Seasonal dishes and sweets are prepared and shared among family — simple, local, tied to what the monsoon land gives. And like every auspicious day, the home is marked with fresh Aipan at the threshold and in the puja room.

## Why it endures

Harela survives because it asks little and means much. It needs no temple and no money — only ten days of attention and a handful of grain. For the Kumaoni diaspora, a basket of grain sown on a city windowsill is sometimes all that remains of Harela — and it is enough.
