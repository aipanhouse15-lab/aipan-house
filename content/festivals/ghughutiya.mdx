**Quick answer:** Ghughutiya — also called Kale Kauva or Ghughuti Tyar — is Kumaon's celebration around Makar Sankranti (Uttarayani). **Makar Sankranti is observed on Friday, 15 January 2027**, following the solar transition on the evening of January 14. Preparations and household customs may span **14–15 January**; check a local panchang for the crow-feeding observance. The ghughuts are sweet fried dough shapes strung into necklaces, and children call the crows — *kale kauva kale, ghughuti mala khale*.

The [Drik Panchang calendar for 2027](https://www.drikpanchang.com/sankranti/makar-sankranti-date-time.html?year=2027&geoname-id=1273294) distinguishes the January 14 evening transition from the January 15 observance. The Bageshwar mela has its own announced programme; do not use the preparation window as a confirmed mela opening date.

Ask a Kumaoni living anywhere in the world what they miss most about winter in the hills and there is a fair chance the answer is a fried piece of sweet dough shaped like a bird. Ghughutiya is Kumaon's most beloved children's festival — the one built entirely around a treat, a necklace, and a conversation with crows.

## What Ghughutiya Is

Ghughutiya is the Kumaoni observance of **Makar Sankranti**, the sun's entry into Makara or Capricorn in the traditional calendar. The season is associated with **Uttarayan**, which gives Uttarayani its name. This calendar association should not be confused with the astronomical winter solstice in December, after which the days begin lengthening in the northern hemisphere.

Across India the Sankranti season has different foods and customs. In Kumaon its distinctive image is the **ghughut**: a sweet, shaped piece of fried dough, made into a necklace and shared with the crows.

The timing carries real weight in the hills. Mid-January is the cold trough of the mountain year — fields resting, snow on the high ridges, the shortest days just past. Makar Sankranti is the turn: from this day the sun strengthens, and the slow climb toward spring and [Phool Dei](/festivals/phool-dei) begins. The festival celebrates that turn with warmth in its most literal forms — deep-fried sweets, morning fires, and a fair on a riverbank.

## Making the Ghughuts

On the eve of Sankranti, kitchens across Kumaon turn out the ghughuts. The dough is simple and rich: wheat flour kneaded with **jaggery dissolved in warm milk or water and a good amount of ghee**, sometimes scented with fennel. The kneaded dough is shaped by hand — and the shapes are the festival's folk art:

- the **ghughuti** itself, a plump little bird
- **damru** — the two-headed drum of Shiva
- **dadim ka phool** — the pomegranate flower
- knives, swords, baskets and rings, according to the house's tradition and the children's demands

The shapes are deep-fried in ghee or oil to a deep brown, cooled, and then **strung onto thread into necklaces** — a ghughut maala — usually alternating with pieces of orange or segments of sugarcane. One necklace per child, hung up overnight, not to be eaten before the crows have had theirs.

The shaping of the dough belongs to the same hand-craft instinct as everything in [our Aipan pages](/aipan/what-is-aipan) — a household making its own ritual objects from kitchen materials, the way Aipan is drawn from rice paste. In many homes the morning of Sankranti also gets a fresh threshold Aipan, of the kind described in [How to Make Aipan](/aipan/how-to-make-aipan).

### A Measured Recipe to Refer To

Household proportions vary. For readers who need measurements, [Rupali Bhandari Karki's published ghughuti recipe](https://madaboutcookingwithrupali.wordpress.com/2021/01/14/ghughutiya-tyar-fest-of-kumaon-love-my-culture-ghughuti-recipe-makar-sankranti-ghughuti/) uses:

- 2 cups whole-wheat flour;
- 1½ cups grated jaggery and 1 cup water for the sweetening liquid;
- ¼ cup sesame seeds and 2 tablespoons fennel seeds;
- a little crushed black pepper and salt;
- 1 tablespoon oil or ghee in the dough, plus oil for frying.

Her method dissolves the jaggery, strains and cools the liquid, mixes the flour with the seeds, and adds the liquid gradually to form a shapeable dough. The dough is divided into small figures and fried until golden brown.

Use that linked recipe for the full measured version; the shaping and necklace suggestions below can also be used with your family's own dough. Add liquid gradually rather than assuming every flour will absorb the same amount. Keep the frying with an adult, and let children help at the shaping table away from hot oil.

## Shaping and Stringing a Ghughut Mala

The first batch does not need to be elaborate. Start with two or three shapes that stay together when lifted. A tray of simple, recognisable pieces is more useful than a delicate bird whose beak breaks off before it reaches the thread.

**A ring** is the easiest starting point. Roll a short rope, curve it into a circle and press the ends together securely. Leave the centre open. Make several rings first so there are easy pieces to thread even if the more ambitious shapes do not survive.

**A bird** can begin as a small oval. Pinch one end for a head and beak, flatten the other end into a tail, and keep the connection between head and body thick enough to hold. A few shallow marks can suggest feathers without making fragile cuts.

**A damru** can be made by shaping a short cylinder with wider ends and a narrower middle. Do not squeeze the waist into a thread-thin join. The drum should still be one solid piece of dough.

Keep each piece reasonably even in thickness. Put unusually thick pieces in a separate batch rather than frying them with fine rings and expecting both to finish together. If a piece is meant to hang, leave an opening while shaping instead of forcing a needle through a brittle fried sweet.

Once the ghughuts have cooled completely, arrange them on a plate before threading. Alternate a few shapes rather than piling every large piece into one heavy necklace. An adult should handle needles and supervise children wearing a mala; take it off for running, play and sleep.

Orange and other fruit are part of many families' malas, as the [Bageshwar district's culture page](https://bageshwar.nic.in/culture-heritage/) records. Add fresh fruit close to the celebration and keep spare dry ghughuts separately. A necklace that has been handled, worn or offered outside should be kept apart from the clean batch meant for eating.

## Common Ghughut-Making Problems

**The dough cracks while shaping.** Work on a small piece first. If it crumbles rather than bending, knead in a little liquid and check again. Large additions are hard to undo. Keep the remaining dough covered while children work so its surface does not dry out.

**The dough sticks and the shapes slump.** Stop adding liquid. Work in a small amount of flour, then try a plain ring before returning to detailed figures. A shape that cannot be lifted from the board is unlikely to become easier to manage in the pan.

**The outside darkens before the middle cooks.** Reduce the heat and try smaller or more evenly shaped pieces. Jaggery dough can colour quickly; the first piece is a useful check before committing the whole tray. Let it cool enough to break open and inspect the centre.

**Parts break off during frying or threading.** Shorten thin tails and handles, strengthen joins, and avoid crowding the pan. A broken piece can still belong on the eating plate; it does not need to be forced into the necklace.

**The finished sweets lose their crispness.** Let them cool fully before putting the dry batch into a clean, airtight container. Keep it away from steam and separate from cut fruit. Do not assign a long shelf life to every batch: milk, fruit, humidity and handling all change how it keeps.

## The Crow at Dawn

Before sunrise on Sankranti morning, children put on their ghughut necklaces, take a piece in hand, go out to the courtyard or rooftop, and **call the crows**:

> *Kale kauva kale, ghughuti mala khale!*
> Black crow, o black crow — come and eat the ghughut necklace!

Other verses follow in Kumaoni, bargaining with the crow: eat the ghughut and bring me a golden necklace; eat the bara and bring me a brother; eat and take my winter's ill-luck with you. The first pieces of every child's maala belong to the crows, set out on the wall or roof or thrown to them. Only after the crows have eaten do the children eat their own — which they do steadily for days, one ghughut at a time off the necklace.

The moment families wait for is **the first crow coming down to the offering**. Sometimes the children have to call again. The custom is the invitation and the sharing; there is no need to promise a child that a bird will arrive at a particular minute.

The call also appears in different wordings. The [Nainital district culture page](https://nainital.nic.in/culture-heritage/) records another version inviting the crow to eat ghughuti and bada. A family's remembered words need not match a printed verse exactly.

## Why Crows

The crow in Hindu tradition is the messenger — of Shani, of the ancestors, of news from far away. Feeding crows at the year's turn feeds the carriers of fortune, and the children's verses say exactly that: the crow eats the sweet, and in exchange carries away misfortune and carries in the good year.

The folk story told in Kumaon gives it a local plot: a king of the hills, a loyal crow who saved the royal child from disaster, and a grateful kingdom that promised the crows a feast every year on this day. Versions differ from valley to valley in the way of all Kumaoni stories; the promise at the end is always the same, and Ghughutiya is the keeping of it.

One version is retold in the National School of Drama's [Ghughuti aur Kau](https://www.brm.nsd.gov.in/event/ghughuti-aur-kau/). In that story, a crow helps save a royal child, and the king establishes an annual offering of sweets to the birds. It is a **folk tale**, not a securely dated account of how the festival began.

That distinction leaves room for the story's real purpose. A child gives away the first sweet before eating from the necklace. The crow becomes a guest rather than something chased from the courtyard, and an ordinary winter morning turns into a conversation between the household and the world outside it.

## Uttarayani and the Bageshwar Mela

<Photo src="/images/ghughutiya-uttarayani-bagnath-2018.jpg" alt="Bagnath temple illuminated during Uttarayani in Bageshwar, photographed in January 2018" caption="Bagnath temple during Uttarayani 2018. Historical photograph, not the 2027 mela." />

Photo: [Bharat Chandola, 13 January 2018](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Bagnath_Temple,_Uttrayani_2018.jpg), [CC BY-SA 4.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/4.0/). Browser screenshot of the photograph.

While the children feed crows at home, the region's great public gathering assembles at **Bageshwar**, where the Saryu and Gomti rivers meet below the ancient Bagnath temple. The **Uttarayani mela** — held around Makar Sankranti — is one of the oldest and largest fairs of the Kumaon Himalaya: a ritual bath at the confluence in the cold of January, darshan at Bagnath, and days of market, music and reunion that once made it the commercial hub of the hill year, when traders came down from the high Bhotiya valleys with wool, salt and borax.

The mela also holds an honoured place in the region's modern history as a gathering point of the **Kuli Begar movement of 1921**, opposing forced porter labour. The account published by the [Uttarakhand Judicial and Legal Academy](https://ujala.uk.gov.in/files/HISTORY_OF_KUMAON_updated.pdf) describes people swearing not to perform Kuli Begar and casting the related registers into the Saryu. This is a history of collective refusal, remembered alongside the fair's religious and trading traditions.

Smaller Uttarayani fairs are held across Kumaon — at Ranibagh, Hanseshwar, and along the Kali in Champawat — but Bageshwar's is the one that draws the region.

Ghughutiya is a Kumaoni household festival; the Bageshwar mela is a public gathering. They belong to the same Uttarayani season, but a family can celebrate the first without travelling to the second. The full regional year is set out in the [Kumaoni festival calendar 2026–27](/festivals/kumaoni-calendar). For Garhwal's November traditions, see [Egaas Bagwal](/festivals/egaas-bagwal).

**Begin with the programme, then book the journey.** Check the [Bageshwar district website](https://bageshwar.nic.in/) for the current mela notice, cultural programme and local advisories. A previous year's poster can show the kind of events the fair includes, but cannot establish the 2027 opening date, closing date or evening performance times.

For a first visit, separate the day into three interests: the **Bagnath temple**, the **riverfront**, and the **market and cultural events**. Trying to finish all three before an onward journey can leave little room for queues or slow movement through the town. Allow time to walk, and check where a vehicle can actually drop you rather than assuming it can reach the temple gate.

January calls for warm layers, especially if you are out before sunrise or stay for evening events. Keep footwear practical for steps and crowded lanes. Ask before photographing worshippers closely, and follow any temple restrictions on photography.

## Celebrating Ghughutiya Away from Kumaon

You do not need a hill courtyard to introduce a child to the festival. Start with the part that travels well: making a few shapes together, naming them, remembering the family's words for the crow and explaining why the first portion is shared.

Make the practical arrangements before the morning rush. Set aside a small offering plate and keep the household's eating batch covered. If you live in a flat, use a place where feeding is permitted and where children can stand safely; a roof edge or balcony railing is not needed to keep the custom meaningful.

When relatives remember different days or different verses, ask them to tell their version rather than settling it by a single social-media caption. Write down the shape names and who taught them. A recipe can be measured, but the family connection often lives in these smaller details: the shape a grandparent always made, the call used in one village, or the orange placed at the centre of the mala.

The season also offers a natural introduction to Kumaon's other household traditions. Children who enjoy shaping ghughuts may enjoy the simple patterns in [easy Aipan designs for beginners](/aipan/easy-aipan-designs-for-beginners). Later in spring, [Phool Dei](/festivals/phool-dei) brings another children's celebration to the doorstep.

## FAQ

### When is Ghughutiya in 2027?

The Makar Sankranti observance is on 15 January 2027; the solar transition takes place on the evening of January 14. Ghughutiya preparations and household customs may span January 14–15. Follow a local panchang or family tradition for the crow-feeding observance, and check the separately announced Uttarayani mela programme before travelling.

### What are ghughuts made of?

Wheat flour kneaded with jaggery (dissolved in warm milk or water) and ghee, sometimes with fennel. The dough is shaped into birds, drums, pomegranate flowers and other figures, deep-fried until brown, and strung into necklaces with orange segments.

### What do children say to the crows on Ghughutiya?

The classic call is "Kale kauva kale, ghughuti mala khale" — black crow, come eat the ghughut necklace. Further verses ask the crow to bring gold, siblings and good fortune in exchange. The crows are fed first; the children eat from their necklaces afterwards.

### Is Ghughutiya on January 14 or January 15 in 2027?

The linked Drik Panchang calendar gives the solar transition on January 14 evening and Makar Sankranti observance on January 15. Ghughutiya's preparation and crow-feeding customs vary by family and locality. Confirm the morning your household follows; the Bageshwar fair has a separate programme.

### What is a ghughut mala?

A necklace of cooled, fried ghughut shapes, often combined with orange or other fruit. Children wear it during the celebration and offer the first pieces to crows. Adults should help with threading and supervise the necklace while it is worn.

### Can I make ghughuts without milk?

Yes. The measured recipe linked in this guide uses jaggery dissolved in water. Household versions may use milk, water, ghee or oil in different combinations. Use a complete recipe and add the sweetening liquid gradually so the dough remains shapeable.

### What is the Uttarayani mela of Bageshwar?

The great Makar Sankranti fair held where the Saryu and Gomti rivers meet at Bageshwar, below the Bagnath temple. It combines a ritual confluence bath, temple darshan and one of the oldest trade fairs of the Kumaon Himalaya, and it was a landmark site of the 1921 coolie-begar protest.

### Is Ghughutiya the same as Makar Sankranti?

Ghughutiya is Kumaon's celebration associated with Makar Sankranti. Its distinctive customs include making ghughut necklaces and calling crows for the first offering. Uttarayani is the wider seasonal name, also used for the public fair at Bageshwar.
